# 2D style: expressive single-line stick figures

Draw a cute, casual black-ink doodle animation pose on white: spare, lively and immediately legible. Create charm through an expressive pose, soft organic curves and clear white space. Keep the same simple strokes suitable for animation, not realistic rendering.

Composition: make the main subjects large, with the action filling most of the usable frame and comfortable white margins. Use a clear side or three-quarter view and one strong line of action. Show the action through the whole pose: tilted torso, reaching arms, bent knees, distinctive silhouettes. Leave negative space between important limbs and around the decisive contact. Do not hide a subject behind another or draw lines through heads and bodies.

People: graceful stick figures with a generous smoothly rounded head and a flowing open spine, two single-line arms and two single-line legs. The body is expressive rather than a stiff vertical pole. Slightly curved limbs end in small natural turns for hands and feet. No joint dots, anatomy, clothes or hair unless the request needs them. People have a plain round head with no default face marks; express intention through the pose. Keep head and body proportions consistent and let the pose express the action.

Animals: appealing compact cartoon silhouettes. Prefer one flowing outer contour joining head and body, instead of stacked overlapping ovals with internal seams. Draw the species-defining ears, muzzle or beak, wings and tail clearly; a tiny eye inside the head. Use lively proportions, rounded curves and the correct limb count. A cat needs a short muzzle with a tiny nose and two short whiskers, pointed ears, and a long tail; a mouse has large rounded ears and a pointed nose. A pelican has a long straight upper bill with a large curved pouch sagging below it, and a folded feather-shaped wing, never a rectangular wing or a generic sharp beak. No skeleton, scribbled fur or gratuitous inner lines. Shape the face rather than using an undifferentiated bean. Use smooth Q/C contours, not angular polygon silhouettes.

Props: recognizable iconic construction with sensible proportions and clear attachments. Use smooth circles, straight rigid edges, and only structural detail needed to identify the object. Keep contacts exact. Every visible line should explain shape or action. No text, arrows, hatching, shading, multiple outlines, motion trails or decorative marks. Use uniform black strokes; draw a short ground line only if it clarifies support. Explicit user appearance requirements override defaults.

Every arm ends at the visible hand contact; every foot reaches its support or pedal. Draw a wing as a graceful organic contour from the shoulder toward its tip, not a mechanical rectangle. Plan the visible outer silhouette before adding inside details; remove construction seams and contours hidden by an opaque foreground shape. Keep scale hierarchy: a person has a generous head around one third of standing height and a compact torso with short single-line limbs, a small traveling object is clearly smaller than the actor, and species-specific proportions matter more than a uniform formula. Small marks that distinguish species or prop identity are welcome; omit marks that add no information. Do not substitute similar objects for the requested subject.

People MUST remain open single-line stick figures. Never add a closed torso outline, shirt, sleeves, trousers, mitten hands or outlined shoes. A simple curved endpoint may suggest a hand or foot. For people, round heads are the only closed body contours. Closed silhouettes belong to robots, animals and props.

Use rounded contours for soft forms and clear corners for pointed ears, bent joints and rigid edges. Keep the correct recognizable shape of each species and prop, stable proportions, and coherent connections. A bold coarse presentation needs enough spacing so separate limbs and important contacts remain readable. People remain single-line stick figures; stroke thickness is set consistently by the renderer.

SMALL-CANVAS CHARACTER DESIGN: draw for the supplied native pixel size, not for a large illustration later reduced. Use compact, cute proportions with a generous rounded head and short lively limbs, keeping the action clear. People still have exactly open single-line torso and limbs, no outlined body, clothes or thick tube limbs. Animal legs are also single open strokes; closed contours are for the animal's head/body and recognizable props. Keep large simple masses, and simplify detail that would collapse into the bold lines. Small face or species marks must remain separated from outer contours. Maintain visible gaps at the actual raster size. Avoid tiny mechanisms, dense spokes or overlapping seams. Structural connections and requested objects must remain recognizable and physically coherent.

Small-canvas simplification must retain the entire requested scene and its action. Keep all actors, props and decisive contacts visible; simplify subordinate detail rather than omitting whole subjects.

ROBOTS: human stick-figure rules apply only to humans. Give a robot a recognizable mechanical head or sensor housing, a simple solid chassis, and articulated mechanical limbs with clear joints. Use sparse box/panel contours and rigid links or short outlined limb segments rather than a human round head on a single-line torso. A humanoid robot may stand and gesture like a person but must retain its mechanical silhouette. Keep it cute and readable without dense circuits or decorative detail.
